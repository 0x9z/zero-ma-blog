I Watched a Packet Get Lost in My Home Network, and It Changed How I Think About the Internet

5 min read by Zero

Request timed out.

Four words, and my ping to a site outside my network just stopped replying — while a ping to my router, one hop away, worked fine. Somewhere between my laptop and the rest of the internet, a packet had left and never come back.

I didn't restart the router. I opened the terminal and started asking the network exactly where things were going wrong.

Step 1: Confirm It's Actually Loss

ping 8.8.8.8

Output:

64 bytes from 8.8.8.8: icmp_seq=1 ttl=118 time=14.2 ms
Request timeout for icmp_seq=2
64 bytes from 8.8.8.8: icmp_seq=3 ttl=118 time=15.8 ms
Request timeout for icmp_seq=4

Not a total outage — intermittent. Every other packet was making it, and every other one was vanishing. That pattern matters: total failure usually means something is down; intermittent loss usually means something is struggling.

Step 2: Find Where It's Actually Happening

A ping only tells you loss exists — not where. For that, I needed to see every hop along the way.

traceroute 8.8.8.8

Output (trimmed):

 1  192.168.1.1        1.2 ms   1.1 ms   1.3 ms
 2  10.10.4.1          8.4 ms   *        9.1 ms
 3  41.202.x.x         14.6 ms  15.0 ms  *
 4  8.8.8.8             14.2 ms  15.8 ms  14.9 ms

Each numbered line is a hop — a router the packet passes through on its way out. The asterisks (*) mark a probe that got no reply. Hop 2, my ISP's local gateway, was where the pattern started. That's the moment this stopped being "the internet is broken" and became "one specific device between me and the internet is dropping packets."

What traceroute Is Actually Doing

This is the part that changed how I see the whole path. `traceroute` doesn't have some special way of "seeing" the route. It exploits a single field in every IP packet: TTL — Time To Live.

Every packet's TTL starts at some number (commonly 64 or 128) and gets decremented by 1 at every router hop. When TTL hits 0, the router drops the packet and sends back an ICMP "Time Exceeded" message to the sender.

traceroute sends a packet with TTL=1
  -> first router drops it, replies "time exceeded" -> that's hop 1

traceroute sends a packet with TTL=2
  -> passes hop 1, second router drops it, replies -> that's hop 2

traceroute sends a packet with TTL=3
  -> passes hop 1 and 2, third router drops it, replies -> that's hop 3
  ...and so on, until TTL is high enough to reach the destination

`traceroute` is deliberately causing packets to expire, one hop further each time, and mapping the path from whoever complains. It's not asking the network "what's your route" — it's provoking every router along the way into revealing itself.

Reading the Result

Hop 1 (my own router) — clean, no loss. Hop 2 (ISP gateway) — intermittent asterisks. Hop 3 — intermittent too. Hop 4 (destination) — fine when it arrived at all.

Loss that starts at one hop and continues through every hop after it usually means the problem lives at that first lossy hop — later hops are just relaying the same already-degraded path, not independently failing on their own.

Confirming It Wasn't My Own Network

ping 192.168.1.1   # my own router — clean, 0% loss
ping 10.10.4.1     # ISP gateway — intermittent loss

My side of the connection — router, Wi-Fi, local switch — was fine. The loss started exactly at the first hop outside my control. That distinction mattered: no amount of restarting my own router was going to fix a problem sitting upstream of it.

Why a Single Lost Packet Isn't Usually a Crisis

TCP — the protocol behind most of what you do online — expects some loss and recovers from it automatically. Every TCP segment is acknowledged; if an acknowledgment doesn't arrive in time, the sender retransmits. A few percent packet loss on a ping often means a slightly slower page load, not a broken connection — you rarely notice it directly.

UDP-based traffic — video calls, some games — has no such safety net built into the protocol itself, which is exactly why intermittent loss shows up as stutters and dropped audio there long before it's visible anywhere else.

What This Moment Taught Me

1. "The internet is slow" is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Ping tells you loss exists. Traceroute tells you where. Without both, you're guessing at a problem that has an exact, findable location.

2. TTL isn't just a header field — it's a tool. I'd seen TTL in packet captures dozens of times as background noise. Watching `traceroute` weaponize it to map an entire path was the moment it stopped being trivia and became something I actually understood the purpose of.

3. Packet loss is normal; the question is where and how much. Networks aren't perfect pipes. The internet is a chain of independent routers doing their best, and TCP was built assuming exactly that.

Quick Reference

# Check for loss to a destination
ping 8.8.8.8

# Map the path and find where loss starts
traceroute 8.8.8.8

# Confirm your own network is clean first
ping <your router's IP>

# Windows equivalent of traceroute
tracert 8.8.8.8

Final Thought

A lost packet used to just mean "the internet glitched" to me. Now it means: open a terminal, ping to confirm it's real, traceroute to find where, and ping each side of that hop to narrow it down further. The internet isn't one thing that's either working or broken — it's dozens of routers in sequence, each one capable of dropping a packet on its own, and TTL is how you go find out which one did.
